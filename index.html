Strides and hill sprints are by feel, not heart rate. They are short (15–20s), relaxed and fast, with full recovery. HR lags so far behind on efforts this brief that chasing a zone on them is meaningless. If he is anywhere near his max HR on strides after an easy run, they are being run far too hard. Relaxed, quick turnover, never a sprint to exhaustion.

Why hills and threshold, not track intervals? Marlow is won or lost on climbing strength and descending control. The uphill reps build the specific engine at low impact (kind to the knee), threshold work raises the sustainable ceiling, and the hilly long runs train the descents. VO2max track intervals would add injury risk without addressing what this course actually demands.

Note on resting HR. The 46 bpm figure is a daytime sitting reading (the watch is not worn asleep), so it is slightly high, making the Easy band a touch conservative at the bottom. Fine, if an easy run feels too hard at the top of Zone 2, run by feel.

The Course, and How to Run It

Marlow climbs about 330m (~1,020ft). Do not be fooled by the flat streets at the start. Run the climbs by effort, use the descents, and never chase pace uphill.

Segment~MilePlan
Start to Marlow Common0–2Steady climb ~200ft. Easy-steady, rightsize effort, do not push early.
First big dipper2–2.580ft down, 130ft up, 80ft down. Controlled descending, strong on the short climb.
Climb to Parmoor (high point)2.5–5Over half the day’s climb. Patient Zone 3–4 effort, do not blow up. Walk the steepest if it is faster.
Descent to Hambleden5–6.5~350ft steep down, often leafy. Controlled-fast, use it, watch footing.
Hambleden Valley (level)6.5–7Flat. Settle to goal effort, recover, re-focus.
Climb to Rockwell End7–8~266ft, the notorious one. Steady-hard, controlled, rhythm and breathing. The crux.
Rolling recovery8–9.5Back of it broken. Recover, stay relaxed.
Second big dipper~10150ft down and straight back up. Controlled, then Marlow Common again.
Final descent to finish11–13.12-mile descent. Let the legs go, strong finish, this is where you get the time back.

Walking the steepest climbs is allowed and often faster. The course notes say so outright. It is a pacing strategy, not a failure.

Do not chase 5:13/km up the climbs. You will pay on the next hill. Hold effort, let pace go on the ups, take it back on the downs. Expect 5:40–6:30+/km climbing and 4:30–4:50/km on the steep descents. The effort-led average is what delivers the time.

The descents are the test for your knee. You have trained them gradually. Run them light and quick, short fast steps, do not over-stride or brake hard. That is what keeps the kneecap happy.

Pacing Frame (two-phase, effort-led)

Phase 1 (start to Parmoor, ~5 miles): Conservative. Everything should feel easy. Bank nothing on the climbs. Reach the top of Parmoor with the tank still full.

Phase 2 (Parmoor to finish): Settle into goal effort through the rolling middle, stay controlled up Rockwell End, then attack the final 2-mile descent for a strong finish.

The One Rule That Matters Most

His injury pattern is behavioural, not structural. PFPS has come from a mentality switch, going from little running straight into a lot, too fast. The conservative ramp is the treatment. If he feels great in week 3 or 6 and wants to add mileage or an extra session, that exact impulse is the risk. Hold the line. The plan is built to get him to the start healthy, not to be the most training he can survive.

Downhill and the Knee

Marlow’s steep descents (a 350ft drop, two big dippers) are the biggest PFPS risk in the whole plan. Downhill running loads the kneecap eccentrically far more than the flat does, and PFPS shows up on descents first.

  • That is why downhill running is built gradually: easy descents through week 3, gently flowing from week 5, controlled-fast only from week 7.
  • Never bomb a descent in training to save time. That is how the knee flares. Light, quick, controlled feet always.
  • If the knee complains on a descent, back off the downhill pace immediately. Do not push through it. It is the early-warning system.

Daily Flags

  • RHR 5+ bpm above ~46 baseline for 2 mornings: drop to easy, no quality.
  • Pain around the kneecap or patellar tendon, especially after descents: stop the session. Two days off if it persists, then return at easy only, on flatter ground.
  • Under 5 hours sleep: downgrade quality to easy.
  • Aches and low motivation 3+ days running: take an unplanned deload.

Session Signals

  • Cannot hit T-pace comfortably: slow 8 sec/km and treat it as sub-threshold. The anchor is conservative by design.
  • Hilly long run falls apart or the knee flares on descents: revise toward Goal B (sub 1:55) rather than forcing it. Better a strong 1:55 on a brutal course than a blown 1:49.
  • Easy pace dropping 15+ sec/km at the same HR over a week: fatigue accumulating, deload or drop the next quality session.

Missed Sessions

Never stack and never chase. A missed easy run is skipped. A missed long run shifts a day or two, or shortens, never doubled up.

If a whole week is missed: do not compress the plan to catch up. Extend, or accept a more conservative goal. Compressing the ramp is exactly what has caused injury before.

On the Long Run Fraction

The long run runs above the usual 30% of weekly volume (up to ~49% in peak week). Deliberate and correct for a low-mileage half: long-run distance is set by the race, not weekly volume. It is kept safe by building gradually, running the easy portions genuinely easy, and introducing the downhills slowly.

Race Week Illness

Mild cold, head only, no fever: race goes ahead.

Chest, fever, or below the neck: do not race. It is a local half, there will be others.
