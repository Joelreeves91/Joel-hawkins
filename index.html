Why race pace is slower than your easy pace. A 100km is run below aerobic threshold for 12 hours. Anything that feels "comfortable" for the first 30km is too fast. 6:30–6:40/km at lower Zone 2 spends almost no glycogen, keeps the gut working and protects the legs for the final 30km, where the race is actually decided. The race pace long runs train that discipline.

Strides and hill strides are by feel, not heart rate. They are 15–20s, relaxed and quick, with full recovery. HR lags too far behind to mean anything on efforts that short.

Verdict: sub 12h is realistic but not guaranteed. The target pace (6:30–6:40/km moving) sits around the slow end of your current easy pace, so the aerobic engine is not the problem. The problem is going from a 10km week to a 40km Saturday and a 22km Sunday without the hip, knee or calves breaking. That is what 44 weeks buys: a slow enough build to make it safe.

What This Block Must Deliver

  • Durability first: 12 weeks of consistent, pain-free easy running and two strength sessions a week before anything ambitious.
  • Long run to 40km: built in steps of 10% or less against the longest run of the previous 30 days (Frandsen 2025).
  • Back-to-back weekends: peaking at 40km + 22km. For a flat 100km this matters more than any single long run (Koop).
  • A trained gut: 60–75g carbs per hour, including real food, practised in every long run from week 13.
  • Race pace discipline: 6:30–6:40/km with walk breaks, rehearsed until it is automatic.

Structure

Monday rest, Tuesday quality, Wednesday strength (plus a short run from week 29), Thursday easy, Friday hips and core, weekend long runs. One quality session a week only, as the ultra guidance prescribes: the back-to-back weekend is the main training tool (Koop). Around 85% of running stays in Zone 2 or below (Seiler, Koop).

No downhill or power-hiking sessions: the course is flat, so they would train the wrong systems.

Race Fuelling

  • Carbs: 60–75g per hour from the first hour. Gels and drink early, real food (rice, potatoes, sandwiches, bananas) from hour 2.
  • Sodium: start at 300–600mg per hour. No sweat data yet: weigh before and after a 2-hour summer long run (1kg lost ≈ 1 litre) to refine fluid and sodium.
  • Fluid: drink to thirst, roughly 400–800ml per hour. Over-drinking without sodium risks hyponatraemia (Noakes).
  • Caffeine: only if tested in training. Most useful after 60km.

Ultra Challenge Thames Path, 100km continuous on the towpath from Putney Bridge to Henley, essentially flat. Pacing (effort led)

0–30km: HR cap 145, 6:30–6:40/km. It will feel far too easy. It is meant to.

30–70km: hold the same effort. Pace can drift a little as HR rises with time, that is normal. Do not chase lost seconds.

70–100km: the real race. Hold pace if you can. Only push in the final 10km if everything is working.

Rest Stops and Walk Breaks

  • Walk through every rest stop while eating. Keep each stop short: refill, grab food, walk out.
  • Between stops, walk 1 minute every 5km to eat and reset. Rehearsed in the long runs from week 30.
  • Have a one-line plan for each stop: what you refill, what you eat.
  • Flat ground means the same muscles work all day. Walk breaks protect pace late on, they are not a weakness.

Kit

  • Only kit worn in at least two long runs, including shoes and socks.
  • Anti-chafe on feet, thighs and anywhere the vest rubs.
  • Head torch and any mandatory items on the 2027 kit list, even if you plan to finish in daylight.

The Rule That Matters Most

Your injuries come from doing too much too soon, not from weakness. PFPS came from jumping volume, and the hip went in the week of your biggest long run and first threshold session. The slow start is the plan. Feeling great in week 6 is not a reason to add a run. Hold the line.

Hip: it has not been assessed. A physio check before or during the first month is strongly recommended, before the long runs start to grow.

Daily Flags

  • RHR 5+ bpm above your normal (~42) for 2 mornings: easy only, no quality.
  • Pain at the front of the hip, groin or kneecap: stop the run. Two days off if it is still there, then return at easy only. Pain that returns twice means physio, not more rest.
  • Under 6 hours sleep before a long run: cut it by a third.
  • Aches and low motivation for 3+ days: take an unplanned deload week.

Session Signals

  • Cannot hold threshold pace inside Zone 4: run to HR, not pace. The pace is a guide.
  • Race pace long runs: if HR at 6:30–6:40/km sits above 150 in the first hour on flat ground, flag it. Repeated in weeks 33–35, switch to Goal B.
  • Back-to-back day 2 still painful after 45min: walk home. Heavy legs are normal, pain is not.
  • Stomach problems at 60g/h: drop to 45g/h, change product, rebuild over 3 long runs.

Missed Sessions

Never stack, never chase. A missed easy run is skipped. A missed Saturday long run is never moved onto Sunday to make a double: run the Sunday as planned and move on.

A missed week: repeat the previous week before continuing. Two or more missed weeks: drop back to the last deload week and rebuild from there. There is enough slack in 44 weeks to absorb this.

On the Long Runs

Long runs pass the 30km marathon cap from week 30. That is correct for a 100km, but no single run goes past 40km (about 4h30): beyond that injury risk rises faster than the benefit, and the back-to-back weekend does the rest (Koop). The weekend carries a large share of weekly volume by design, which is why weekday running stays short and easy.

Race Week Illness

Head cold, no fever: race, at the conservative end of the pacing plan.

Chest, fever or stomach bug: do not start. 12 hours ill is not worth it.
